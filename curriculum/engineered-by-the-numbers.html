Engineered by the Numbers

Unit plan for Principles of Engineering. Grades 10 to 12. About 35 class periods of 58 minutes. Teams of 2 or 3.

The goal of this unit is to help students learn how to apply the fundamental math and physics of mechanical engineering through a series of example problems.

Each example problem is a machine element students can build and test: a gear reducer, a screw jack, a friction brake and a loaded shaft. The last problem combines all of them into a working belt conveyor. Simulations run alongside every problem so students predict, check and explain before and after they build. Every formula on this page is checked against a published engineering or physics reference, and each one links to its source.

The project list comes from a short Instagram reel by aerofluid.in[32]. The reel names five projects and closes with a design-process framework. The idea is good, but the reel is labeled as AI content and several of its on-screen formulas are wrong, so this unit rebuilds each project from references and changes the order. The corrections are listed in Notes on the source video.

Planning assumptions

The unit is written for Principles of Engineering, grades 10 to 12, with 58-minute periods and teams of 2 or 3. The calculations assume Algebra 1 and some geometry. Trig, cube roots and the physics ideas are taught in short supplements, so a student who has not taken physics can still do the core work.

No robotics experience is assumed. Students who have built robots will recognize gears and motors and often make good lab partners, but every idea here is taught from the beginning, and the labs use ordinary classroom materials.

Where it fits in the course. PLTW describes Principles of Engineering as a survey course covering mechanisms, the strength of materials and structures, automation and kinematics[37]. Its published outline pairs a mechanisms unit, where students learn that friction losses reduce efficiency, with a materials and structures unit on beam deflection, truss forces and material testing[37]. PLTW has since released a revised course outline[38], so check which version your school follows. This unit goes deeper into the mechanisms and statics strands. If your students have already done simple machines, gear ratios or truss statics, modules 0 and 1 and the truss warm-ups run as review and you can reclaim a few periods.

Units. The unit works in SI because the physics references and the gear module system use it. Many engineering course materials and classroom kits use US customary units, and the Truss Stress Visualizer uses a half-inch grid on a 6-inch span with loads in newtons, so supplement S1 covers conversions.

Shorter version. Run modules 0 to 3 and replace the full capstone with a simpler gear-and-pulley lift. Each module separates core work from blue extension boxes.

Why the order changed

The reel starts with shaft design. A shaft calculation needs torque from power and speed, bending moments from support reactions, two kinds of stress, and a rule for combining them. It also needs to know what is loading the shaft, which in a real machine is a gear or a pulley. That is four or five new ideas in the first project. Moving the shaft to fourth place means every one of those ideas has already been taught once in a simpler setting.

There is a nice contrast between modules 2 and 3. A screw jack should be self-locking so it holds a load when you let go. A brake should not be self-locking, or it grabs. Students see the same friction math push the design in opposite directions.

The design process students follow

This is the reel's closing framework with one change. It adds a return path, because a test that disagrees with the calculation is the most useful moment in the whole process. NGSS asks students to design, evaluate and refine solutions against prioritized criteria and trade-offs, and to use models to predict how a design will perform[29]. Each module ends with a small version of this loop. The capstone runs the full loop.

Tools and simulations

Simulations carry a lot of this unit. Two of them already exist as your own classroom tools, the Gear Train Workbench[39] and the Truss Stress Visualizer[40]. Six more are built into this page. The section below maps where each one is used, suggests extensions to your two tools that would close gaps in the unit, and proposes new tools worth building.

One routine for every tool: predict, run, explain

The Workbench's challenge mode already models the right habit. Students enter a predicted output before the readout appears, and each run hides the readout again, so every attempt needs a fresh prediction[39]. Every interactive on this page now has the same switch. Turn on Predict first, and the results column goes blank until the student types a number and presses Check. The tool then reports the model value and the percent difference. Moving any slider hides the results again.

The routine for each example problem is the same across the unit. Students solve by hand with units on every line, predict in the tool, run it, and write one sentence about any gap. When there is a physical lab, a third column gets added, measured. Prediction, model and measurement disagreeing in different ways is where most of the physics conversation happens.

Gear Train Workbench: suggested extensions

Today the Workbench builds simple, idler and compound trains on two planes, drives them with a motor at a set speed and torque, reports ratio, output speed, output torque and direction, and treats the train as ideal, with power in equal to power out[39]. These additions, listed in the order I would build them, would let it carry more of modules 1 and 5.

  1. Efficiency per mesh. An optional efficiency value on each mesh, default 1.00, so output torque becomes η · i · τin and the readout shows power lost. Students enter the efficiency they measured in period 5 and watch it compound across stages. Keep the ideal mode as the default so the current challenges still work.
  2. Motor curve instead of a fixed speed. Let the motor be described by stall torque and no-load speed, and let the Load tool apply a torque. The output speed then drops under load along the straight-line motor curve[43]. This is the single idea most students miss in the capstone, and the motor interactive on this page is a prototype of it.
  3. Gear geometry. A module setting per gear, with pitch diameter and center distance shown for each mesh (d = zm, a = m(z1 + z2)/2[6][7]). An SVG export of shaft-hole positions would feed straight into laser cutting a gear plate with your ULS Laser Ready workflow.
  4. Tooth forces and shaft loads. For each mesh, show Ft = 2T/d and Fr = Ft tan 20°[9]. A small export of the loads on each shaft would let a shaft tool pick up where the Workbench stops.
  5. A lift task on the Load tool. A spool radius and hanging mass, with required torque, lift speed and lifting power (mgv). That makes the module 0 power lab and the module 1 lift lab directly checkable.
  6. Belt and chain stages. A non-meshing stage where two pulleys or sprockets on a belt turn the same direction, for the conveyor drive.
  7. A challenge pack for this unit. For example: reach 95 rpm within 5% from a 300 rpm motor using listed sizes; build a 12 : 1 reducer in two stages; or a challenge with a hidden mesh efficiency that students must infer from the output torque.

Truss Stress Visualizer: suggested extensions

The Visualizer solves pin-jointed trusses by the method of joints, colors tension and compression, sizes lines by utilization, flags the predicted first member to fail, and has a free-body inspector with a ΣF = 0 check. It mirrors a Vernier tester load case, includes material presets, and warns when the prediction passes the tester's 1000 N load cell. Your notes list buckling as the planned phase 2[40]. Within this unit it supports the statics parts of modules 0 and 4. These additions would stretch it further.

  1. Buckling (your phase 2). A length-dependent limit for compression members, using Euler's critical load Pcr = π²EI/(KL)² with K = 1 for pinned ends[41]. Euler only applies to long, slender members, and short ones fail by other means[41], so the tool should say which applies. This also connects to the screw jack, since a long screw under load is a column and designers check it for buckling[5].
  2. A stress view. Given each member's cross-section, show σ = F/A in MPa beside the force, and compare it with the material's strength instead of a per-member force limit[23]. This ties the truss work to the stress ideas in modules 2 and 4 and to supplement S10.
  3. A beam mode. One member on two supports carrying point loads, with reactions (Wb/L and Wa/L) plus shear and bending moment diagrams[12]. A pin-jointed truss cannot show bending, and bending is exactly what module 4 needs, so this is the natural bridge from truss statics to shafts.
  4. Larger workspace and unit toggle. A span larger than the 6-inch tester, and a switch between inches and millimeters and between newtons and pound-force. That would let teams model a conveyor stand.

New tools worth building

Listed by how much each would help the unit, most useful first.

  1. Shaft and Beam Workbench. Place bearings and loads on a shaft, import gear forces from the Workbench or belt tensions from the conveyor tool, and see reactions, the bending moment diagram, torque, combined stress and the diameter needed for a chosen safety factor[13][14]. A switch between the Tresca and von Mises rules, and a keyway toggle that shows the local stress rise[15], would cover the extension work. Module 4 is the hardest module and this tool would link your two existing ones.
  2. Motor and Drive Matcher. Enter a motor's stall torque and no-load speed from its datasheet, add a gear ratio and efficiency, and set the load. The tool plots the operating point on the motor curve, flags the duty range[42], and solves for the ratio that hits a target loaded speed. The capstone depends on this, and the prototype is on this page.
  3. Friction and Screw Lab. A ramp to find friction coefficients (tan θ, with a place to enter measured angles)[22], joined to a screw jack with square or Acme thread and optional collar friction[4][5]. Students see measured friction flow into lift torque and self-locking.
  4. Flywheel Brake Lab. Students enter frame counts from slow-motion video, and the tool plots speed against time, fits the slowdown, and computes braking torque, friction coefficient and energy[25][27]. An energy bar that empties from kinetic into heat, with the Q = mcΔT upper limit, would carry the module 3 argument[26].
  5. Conveyor Designer. The capstone integrator. Students enter the requirements sheet, the tool walks through belt pull, pulley, ratio, motor check and shaft side load, and each requirement shows pass or fail. A printable design record, like the one in the Truss Stress Visualizer[40], would serve as the capstone's calculation package.
  6. Units and formula checker. Students type a chain of calculation steps with units, and the tool flags mismatches such as N·mm mixed with N·m, or rpm used where rad/s is needed. It directly supports supplements S1 and S2, where most early errors happen.
  7. A shared prediction log. One export format for every tool, with a row for predicted, model and measured values plus the percent differences. A single class spreadsheet could then collect results from all six modules.

Build conventions worth keeping. Your tools already share habits that suit this unit: one self-contained file that runs on a Chromebook, predict before reveal, a "show the working" view, keyboard control, respect for reduced-motion settings, and export to an image or report[39][40]. The Visualizer also tests its solver against hand-calculated cases before release[40]. The worked checks on this page are ready-made test cases for each new tool, and each has already been run through the code behind the interactives.

0. Toolkit

What do engineers measure when a machine moves?

Periods 1 to 3. The goal is shared vocabulary and a diagnostic so you know which students need which supplements before the math gets heavier.

Required concepts

  • SI units and prefixes. Converting mm to m, and N·mm to N·m. Supplement S1.
  • Mass versus weight. Weight is the force of gravity, W = mg, with g = 9.81 m/s². S6.
  • Torque as force times perpendicular lever arm, τ = rF, measured in N·m[24]. S7.
  • Rotational speed in rpm and in rad/s, ω = 2πN/60.
  • Power in straight-line motion, P = Fv, and in rotation, P = τω[27]. S9.
  • Efficiency as useful power out divided by power in, η = Pout/Pin.
τ = rF    ω = 2πN/60    P = τω N in rpm, ω in rad/s, P in watts[24][27]

OpenStax works an example that is a good sanity check for students: a 9.0 × 10⁴ W boat engine at 300 rev/min turns at 31.4 rad/s, which gives a shaft torque of about 2,865 N·m[27].

Concept animation: torque is force times the perpendicular lever arm New · for review

Watch the blue r⊥ shrink as the push swings toward the handle. Torque is largest when the push is at right angles to the wrench, and zero when it points along it. This is the door-hinge question from period 2: pushing near the hinge makes r small.

Activities

  • Period 1Hook and diagnostic. Show the reel if you want to (see the teacher note below), then ask teams to sketch what they think is inside a car jack and a garage door opener. Give a 10-item diagnostic covering unit conversion, solving a formula for one variable, ratios, reading a right triangle, and the difference between mass and weight. Use the results to assign supplements. Introduce the design-process diagram.
  • Period 2Torque lab. Balance a meter stick on a pivot, hang known masses, and pull with a spring scale at three distances. Teams record force and distance and notice that the product stays constant for balance. That product is torque. Finish with the door-hinge question: why is a door easy to open at the handle and hard near the hinge?
  • Period 3Power lab. Lift a known mass with a string wound on a spool, either by hand crank or with a small motor. Measure height, time and spool rpm (count turns in slow-motion phone video). Compute P = mgh/t, then τ = P/ω, then check it against weight times spool radius. Students start their formula card, a single page they add to in every module and may use on quizzes.

Materials. Meter sticks and pivots, slotted masses, spring scales (a 0 to 50 N range covers most of this unit), string, spools or pulleys, a hand crank or small gearmotor, stopwatches or phone slow-motion video.

Teacher note on the reel. If you show the reel as a hook, pause before the formulas. Several are wrong on screen. Period 26 turns this into a deliberate error-hunt activity once students know enough to catch the mistakes themselves.

1. Speed reducer

How can a small fast motor lift something heavy?

Periods 4 to 9. Students trade speed for torque with spur gears, predict the output, and measure it. Ratios need only multiplication and division, which makes gears a gentle first example problem for every student.

Required concepts

  • Speed ratio. For a driver with z1 teeth turning N1 and a driven gear with z2 teeth turning N2, the ratio is i = N1/N2 = z2/z1. A ratio above 1 reduces speed[8]. S3.
  • Torque goes up by the same ratio, because power in equals power out for ideal gears. τ1ω1 = τ2ω2, so τ2 = iτ1. Real gears lose some power, so τ2 = ηiτ1[27].
  • Compound trains. When two stages share a shaft, the overall ratio is the product of the stage ratios, and the overall efficiency is the product of the stage efficiencies. Both follow directly from the definitions above.
  • Gear geometry. The module m sets tooth size. Reference (pitch) diameter is d = zm, pitch is πm, and gears that mesh must share a module[6][34]. Center distance is half the sum of the two pitch diameters[7].
  • Pressure angle. Most modern gears use 20°, although 14.5° gears were once common[6].
  • Tooth forces. The tangential force that carries the torque is Ft = 2T/d. The radial force that pushes the gears apart is Fr = Ft tan α. Spur gears have no axial force[9]. These two forces are what bend the shaft in module 4. S4.
i = z₂/z₁    τ₂ = η · i · τ₁    d = zm    a = m(z₁ + z₂)/2 [6][7][8]
Ft = 2T/d    Fr = Ft tan α α = 20° for most gears[9]

Concept animation: zoom in on two teeth at the mesh New · for review

The dashed pitch circles roll on each other without slipping, which is why the speed ratio equals the tooth ratio. The teeth push along the blue line of action, tilted by the pressure angle. The tangential part Ft = 2T/d turns the gear; the radial part Fr = Ft tan α pushes the two shafts apart, and it is part of what bends the shaft in module 4. Forces use the worked check: 0.5 N·m on a 24 mm pinion. The inset shows where the zoom sits on the whole pair.

Worked check. A 12-tooth pinion drives a 36-tooth gear, module 2 mm, with 0.5 N·m on the pinion. Then i = 3, d1 = 24 mm, d2 = 72 mm and the center distance is 48 mm. The tangential force is 2(0.5)/0.024 = 41.7 N and the radial force is 41.7 × tan 20° = 15.2 N. KHK's own example uses 17 and 34 teeth at module 1 for a 25.5 mm center distance, which students can check the same way[36].

Activities

  • Period 4Count it. Mount two gears, mark one tooth on each, and turn the driver 10 full turns. Count output turns and compare with z2/z1. Repeat with the gears swapped. Ask why the driven gear turns the opposite way, and what an idler gear would change. Then open the Gear Train Workbench in challenge mode. Teams predict output speed and direction for a simple train and an idler train before running, and use "Show the working" to find any wrong step[39].
  • Period 5The trade-off lab. Put a spool on the output shaft and lift a hanging mass. Turn the input with a crank of known radius and read the crank force on a spring scale. Compute input and output torque, then efficiency. Teams usually find it well below 1, which starts a good conversation about friction in bearings and teeth. Build the same train in the Workbench and compare. The Workbench gives the ideal torque (in N·cm), so the ratio of measured to ideal torque is the efficiency.
  • Period 6Geometry. Introduce module, pitch diameter and center distance with the interactive above. Teams measure real gears with calipers, back-calculate the module, and check that their gear plate hole spacing matches a = m(z1 + z2)/2.
  • Period 7Tooth forces. Right-triangle trig supplement for anyone who needs it. Compute Ft and Fr for the period 5 setup. Draw the forces on a free-body sketch of the output shaft and save it for module 4.
  • Period 8Design brief. From the gears in the bin, specify a two-stage reducer that hits a target output speed within 5% and lifts a target mass. Each team builds the design in the Workbench first and attaches the exported report to a calc sheet with units on every line and a predicted efficiency based on period 5.
  • Period 9Build and test. Measure output speed and lifting ability. Record predicted versus measured and write two sentences on the biggest gap.

Extension: will the teeth break? The Lewis equation treats one tooth as a small cantilever beam loaded at its tip, σ = Ft/(bmY), where b is face width and Y is the Lewis form factor from a table based on tooth count and pressure angle[10][11]. It ignores stress concentration at the tooth root and assumes one tooth carries the whole load[11], so it is a first estimate. Students look up Y for their pinion and compare the stress with the plastic's strength from its datasheet. This is a good task for anyone 3D printing gears.

Materials. Any classroom gear kit (LEGO Technic, VEX or similar) or 3D-printed gears, shafts and bearings or bushings, a crank, spool and string, slotted masses, spring scales, calipers.

Safety. Gear meshes are pinch points. Power off before adjusting, tie back hair, and no lanyards or loose sleeves near the mesh. Keep hanging masses low over the floor, with feet clear.

2. Screw jack

Why doesn't a car jack slide back down when you let go?

Periods 10 to 15. A screw thread is an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder. Students measure friction, compute the torque to lift a load, and find out when a screw will hold its load on its own.

Required concepts

  • Friction. Friction opposes sliding, f = μN, with a static coefficient that is usually larger than the kinetic one. A block that slides down a ramp at constant speed gives μk = tan θ[22]. S8.
  • Friction angle. Define φ by tan φ = μ. S4.
  • Pitch, lead and starts. Lead is how far the nut moves in one turn, l = n × pitch for an n-start thread[4].
  • Mean diameter and lead angle. For an ideal square thread of depth p/2, the mean diameter is dm = d − p/2 and the root diameter is d − p[1][35]. The lead angle comes from unrolling one turn of thread into a triangle, tan λ = l/(πdm)[2].
  • Torque to raise a load. For a square thread with collar friction ignored:
T = W dm2 · l + πμdmπdm − μl  =  W dm2 tan(φ + λ) Shigley eq. 8-1 form[1][35]
  • The two forms are the same formula. The second one uses the tangent addition identity with tan φ = μ and tan λ = l/(πdm), and it is easier for students to picture.
  • Efficiency is the work done lifting the load in one turn divided by the work put in, η = Wl/(2πT)[3]. Standard Acme screws with lead angles of about 2° to 5° and a friction coefficient of 0.15 run at roughly 18% to 36% efficiency[5].
  • Self-locking. The screw holds its load with no torque applied when πμdm > l, which is the same as μ > tan λ, or φ > λ[3]. Most car screw jacks are self-locking[5]. Vibration can lower the effective friction and let a screw back-drive[5].
  • Handle force. F = T/R for a handle of radius R. With no friction, work in equals work out, so the ideal handle force is Wl/(2πR).
  • Stress in the screw. The screw is pushed along its length, so the compressive stress is load over area, σ = F/A = 4W/(πdr²)[23]. S10.

Concept animation: when does the block slide? New · for review

This is the period 10 friction lab. The board tilts until tan θ passes μs, which is the same as θ passing the friction angle φ = tan⁻¹ μs (dashed red). Until then friction grows to match the pull down the slope. Once sliding starts, kinetic friction μkN takes over. The screw jack holds its load by the same rule, with the thread's lead angle in place of the tilt.

Concept animation: a thread is a ramp wrapped around a cylinder New · for review

This is the period 11 paper activity, animated. The triangle's base is one trip around the dowel, π dm. Its height is the lead. Wrapped up, the slanted edge becomes one turn of thread, so the lead angle comes straight from the triangle: tan λ = l/(π dm). The default lead is larger than the class screw's 4 mm so the slope is easy to see; set l = 4 to see how flat a real single-start thread is.

Worked check. Load 2000 N on a single-start square thread with d = 20 mm, pitch 4 mm and μ = 0.15. Lead is 4 mm, dm = 18 mm, λ = 4.05° and φ = 8.53°. Torque is 4.02 N·m, so a 300 mm handle needs 13.4 N. Efficiency is 31.7%, inside the published range above. Since φ > λ, the screw is self-locking. Compressive stress on the 16 mm root is 9.9 MPa.

Activities

  • Period 10Friction lab. Tilt a board until a block starts to slide, then find the angle where a tapped block slides at constant speed. The tangent of each angle estimates μs and μk[22]. Test steel on steel dry and with a drop of oil. Teams post results so the class builds its own friction table.
  • Period 11Unroll the thread. Cut a paper right triangle with base πdm and height equal to the lead, then wrap it around a dowel of diameter dm. The slanted edge becomes one turn of a helix. Measure a threaded rod's pitch with calipers or a pitch gauge and compute its lead angle.
  • Period 12Derive and compute. Start from pushing a block up a ramp with friction, then connect the ramp to the unrolled thread. Compute the torque for the class screw at three loads.
  • Period 13Test. Use a threaded-rod jack (rod, nut, a base and a handle) or a small commercial screw jack. Lift sandbags or masses, measure the handle force with a spring scale at a known radius, and measure lift per turn. Compute efficiency and compare with the prediction.
  • Period 14Self-locking. Predict whether your screw holds its load, then test by releasing the handle. Compare a single-start rod with a multi-start lead screw if you have one (3D printer lead screws are often multi-start). Add a stress check on the root diameter.
  • Period 15Mini brief. Specify diameter, pitch, starts and handle length for a jack that lifts a set load with no more than a set hand force and stays self-locking. Short quiz on modules 1 and 2.

Extension: real threads. The formula above is for square threads. Acme and V threads have a flank angle that wedges the nut and raises the effective friction, which some texts write as μ sec β for a half-angle β[5]. A jack with a thrust collar also loses torque to collar friction, which adds its own term[4]. Students can compare their measured efficiency with a square-thread prediction and explain the gap. A long screw under load is also a column, and designers check it for buckling[5]. Euler's critical load, Pcr = π²EI/(KL)², shows why doubling the unsupported length cuts the buckling load to a quarter[41].

Materials. Threaded rod with matching nuts, a base plate, a handle bar, calipers or a thread pitch gauge, spring scales, sandbags or slotted masses, a ramp board and friction blocks, light oil.

Safety. Nobody puts a hand or foot under a raised load, ever. Lift sandbags or masses only, keep lift heights low, and set a maximum class load before testing. Gloves for handling threaded rod.

3. Brake

Where does the motion go when a wheel stops?

Periods 16 to 20. Students build a lever-operated shoe brake, predict how fast it stops a spinning wheel, and track the energy into heat.

Required concepts

  • Friction at the shoe. Pressing the shoe on the drum with normal force N produces a friction force μN at the drum surface[22]. The braking torque is that force times the drum radius, Tb = μNr[17].
  • Lever balance. Take moments about the lever pivot. In the simplest layout the friction force lines up with the pivot, so it has no moment arm, and Pl = Nx, where P is the hand force at distance l and the shoe sits at distance x[16]. S7.
  • Short shoe assumption. When the shoe covers a small angle of the drum, the contact pressure is treated as uniform and the friction acts at the middle of the shoe[17].
  • Rotational kinetic energy, KE = ½Iω²[25]. For a solid disk I = ½mr², and for a thin hoop such as a bicycle rim I is close to mr²[31].
  • Work and energy. The brake does work Tbθ as the wheel turns through angle θ, and that work equals the kinetic energy removed[27]. With constant braking torque, the wheel slows at α = Tb/I and stops in time t = Iω₀/Tb.
  • Heat. The energy becomes internal energy of the drum and pad. Temperature rise follows Q = mcΔT[26]. Truck brakes on long downhill runs overheat for exactly this reason[33]. S11.
N = Pl/x    Tb = μNr    KE = ½Iω²    t = Iω₀/Tb    ΔT ≤ KE/(mc) [16][25][26][27]

The temperature line is written as an upper limit on purpose. It assumes all of the energy stays in the drum. Some goes into the pad and the air, so a measured rise will be lower.

Concept animation: zoom in on the brake shoe and its pivot New · for review

Start with the pivot on the friction line: friction has no moment arm, so N = Pl/x = 60 N, as in the worked check. Then raise the pivot 20 mm (the extension box below). Now friction has an arm a. One way round it lifts the shoe (56.1 N). The other way it drags the shoe on and helps the hand (64.5 N), which is called self-energizing. P = 20 N, l = 300 mm, x = 100 mm and μ = 0.35 throughout.

Worked check. A solid steel disk of 8 kg and radius 150 mm spins at 300 rpm, so I = 0.09 kg·m², ω = 31.4 rad/s and KE = 44.4 J. A 20 N hand force on a lever with l/x = 3 gives N = 60 N. With μ = 0.35 the friction force is 21 N and Tb = 3.15 N·m. The disk stops in 0.90 s after about 2.2 turns. Steel's specific heat is 452 J/(kg·°C)[26], so the whole disk warms by at most 0.012 °C. That tiny number surprises students and leads to the next question: why do brakes get hot anyway? The heat is concentrated in a thin surface layer and in the much smaller pad, and a real vehicle absorbs far more energy in each stop.

Activities

  • Period 16Energy in a spinning wheel. Spin a flywheel or a bicycle wheel on a stand, measure rpm from slow-motion video, and compute kinetic energy. Discuss why the rim of a bicycle wheel is closer to a hoop than a disk.
  • Period 17Build the lever. Teams make a hand lever with a pad (wood with a rubber or cork face works) and choose a pivot position. Hanging a mass on the lever end gives a repeatable hand force. Predict N and Tb.
  • Period 18Stopping tests. Spin to a measured speed, apply the hanging mass, and time the stop on video. Calculate Tb = Iω₀/t from the data, then back-calculate μ and compare it with the ramp values from period 10.
  • Period 19Heat. Measure pad and drum surface temperatures with an IR thermometer before and after ten stops in a row. Compare with the upper limit. Teams write where the rest of the energy went.
  • Period 20Design memo. Size the lever so a set hand force stops the wheel within a set time, with test data attached.

Extension: moving the pivot. When the pivot is not on the friction line, the friction force adds a moment of its own. The lever balance becomes Nx − Fa = Pl, where a is the offset and its sign depends on which way the drum turns[16]. For one direction the friction helps press the shoe on, which is called self-energizing. Push it too far and the required hand force drops to zero, a self-locking brake that grabs. Designers keep the geometry short of that point[17]. Students test both directions of rotation with an offset pivot and explain the difference in stopping time.

Materials. A flywheel on a bearing shaft or a bicycle wheel on a stand, wood and rubber for the lever and pad, a hinge or bolt for the pivot, slotted masses, phone slow-motion video, IR thermometer, kitchen scale.

Safety. Set a class maximum speed for the wheel. Safety glasses on everyone nearby, fingers clear of spokes, and assume the pad and drum surface are hot after repeated stops.

4. Transmission shaft

Why does a slightly thicker shaft get so much stronger?

Periods 21 to 26. The shaft pulls together torque, moments, reactions and stress. Loads come straight from the gear forces in module 1 or a belt pulley.

Required concepts

  • Torque from power and speed, T = P/ω = 60P/(2πN)[27].
  • Where the bending comes from. A gear or pulley pushes sideways on the shaft (the Ft and Fr from module 1, or belt tension in module 5)[9], and the bearings push back.
  • Reactions and bending moment. For a load W at the middle of a span L between two bearings, each bearing carries W/2 and the largest bending moment is WL/4 at the center. For a load at distance a from one support and b from the other, the reactions are Wb/L and Wa/L[12].
  • Bending stress in a solid round shaft, σ = 32M/(πd³), and twisting (shear) stress, τ = 16T/(πd³)[13].
  • The cube law. Both stresses depend on d³, so doubling the diameter cuts the stress to one eighth. S5.
  • Combining the two. The maximum shear stress (Tresca) theory gives τmax = 16√(M² + T²)/(πd³)[13]. The allowable value is Sy/(2n) for yield strength Sy and safety factor n[14].
  • Fatigue, described in words only. A rotating shaft with a steady side load sees its surface go from tension to compression every revolution, and fatigue is the most common way shafts fail. Keyways raise local stress, often by a factor of 2 or more[15].
  • Keys, couplings and bearings, chosen by shaft size and load from a catalog.
M = WL/4    σ = 32M/(πd³)    τ = 16T/(πd³) [12][13]
d = ∛[ 16√(M² + T²) / (π τallow) ]    τallow = Sy/(2n) maximum shear stress theory[13][14]

Concept animation: why the cube law: more material, farther out New · for review

The driving question for module 4. When d grows, there is more material to carry the load (area goes as d²), and that material sits farther from the middle, where bending loads it most (lever arm goes as d). Multiply the two and strength goes as d³, so doubling d cuts the stress to one eighth. The shading shows bending stress in the section: zero on the dashed middle line, largest at the edges.

Worked check. Reuven's published example uses M = T = 1.0 × 10⁶ N·mm, Sy = 400 MPa and n = 2. The allowable shear stress is 100 MPa and the required diameter is 41.6 mm[14]. Students should reproduce that number by hand before trusting their own calc sheet. The same source notes that the distortion-energy (von Mises) theory gives a slightly smaller shaft, while Tresca is more conservative[14].

Concept animation: why a turning shaft fails by fatigue New · for review

The side load always bends the shaft the same way, so the top is squeezed and the bottom is stretched. The shaft turns, so every point on its surface takes turns being on top and on the bottom. The trace is that point's stress: one full tension and compression cycle per turn. At 300 rpm that is 18,000 cycles an hour, which is why fatigue, not a single overload, is the usual way shafts fail. Keyways make it worse by concentrating the stress.

Activities

  • Period 21Loads on a shaft. Pull out the gear-force sketch from period 7. Start in the Truss Stress Visualizer. Build a small truss with a joint at the center of the deck and choose the Tester load case, which pulls straight down at the deck center[40]. Use the Inspect tool on the joints at the pin and the roller and ask what the supports must push back with. With a centered load, each support carries half of it. Then draw the full free-body diagram of the output shaft with two bearings and find the bearing reactions by taking moments, just like the lever in module 0. A gear at the middle of the shaft splits its load the same way.
  • Period 22Bending lab. Support wooden dowels of two or three diameters on two blocks and hang masses at the center. Record sag, then load to failure. Plot failure load against d and against d³ and ask which graph is straighter.
  • Period 23Torsion lab. Clamp one end of a dowel, twist the other with a lever and spring scale, and record the torque at failure. Compare torque capacity with d³ again.
  • Period 24Combined loading. Use the interactive to explore, then size a shaft by hand for a given gear load and power with n = 2, using a yield strength from a real datasheet. Check the Reuven example first.
  • Period 25Components. Choose bearings or pillow blocks and a coupling that fit the computed diameter from a supplier catalog. Discuss why a keyway or set-screw flat is a weak spot, and why many classroom kits use hex shafts instead of round shafts with keys.
  • Period 26Error hunt and checkpoint. Watch the source reel and find the formula errors (see Notes on the source video). Then a checkpoint quiz on modules 1 to 4.

Materials. Wooden dowels in two or three diameters, support blocks, slotted masses and a hanger, a clamp and lever for twisting, spring scales, a ruler or dial indicator for sag, bearing and coupling catalogs, and optionally 3D-printed test shafts.

Safety. Dowels splinter when they break. Safety glasses on, drape a towel over the specimen, keep masses low over the floor, and stand to the side rather than over the test.

5. Belt conveyor capstone

Can your team design a conveyor that meets a spec on the first build?

Periods 27 to 35. Teams run the full design process on one machine that contains every earlier module. A motor drives a gear reduction (module 1) that turns a drive pulley on a shaft (module 4) carried by bearings. Friction between belt and bed sets the load (module 2). A hold or stop feature is optional for inclined designs (modules 2 and 3).

Design brief

Move boxes of a set mass at a set spacing and a target belt speed, within 10%, over a length of 1 to 2 m. Use a motor from the class inventory. Guard every pinch point where the belt meets a pulley and include an easy-to-reach power switch. You set the numbers for each class; the defaults in the interactive below are a reasonable starting point.

Required concepts

  • Load per meter. For boxes of mass mbox at spacing s, the average product mass on the belt is m′p = mbox/s. Throughput is 60v/s boxes per minute.
  • Belt pull. For a belt sliding on a flat bed, the effective tension is friction on the belt and product plus the force to lift the product. The PackagingConveyor formula, rewritten in SI with masses per meter, is below[19]. Their typical friction factors are 0.03 to 0.06 on a UHMW slider bed and 0.15 to 0.30 on steel without UHMW[19], so students should measure their own with the ramp method.
Te = μ g (m′b + m′p) L + m′p g H L = belt length on the bed, H = lift[19]
  • This is a first estimate. The friction term uses full weight, which slightly overestimates friction on an incline, so it errs on the safe side. Industrial bulk conveyors use the longer CEMA method, which adds idler, return-belt, cleaner and acceleration terms[18].
  • Belt pull does not depend on speed. Speed enters at the power step[19].
  • Power. Required power is effective tension times belt speed, P = Tev[18]. PackagingConveyor recommends multiplying by a service factor of 1.5 for normal duty before choosing a motor[19]. Dividing by the drive efficiency measured in module 1 accounts for gear losses.
  • Pulley torque and speed. Torque at the drive pulley is Te × D/2. Belt speed is the pulley's surface speed, so the pulley turns at N = 60v/(πD) rpm. The gear ratio needed is motor speed divided by pulley speed.
  • Tight and slack sides. The belt is tighter coming onto the drive pulley than leaving it, and T1 = Te + T2[18]. With 180° of wrap both spans pull the pulley the same way, so the pulley shaft carries T1 + T2 as a side load. That load goes into the module 4 shaft check.

Extension: will the belt slip? The capstan (Euler-Eytelwein) equation limits the tension ratio a pulley can hold before slipping, T1/T2 ≤ eμθ, with the wrap angle θ in radians[21]. The most pull a drive pulley can transmit is T2(eμθ − 1)[20], so the minimum slack-side tension is Te/(eμθ − 1). That tension comes from how tightly the team adjusts the belt. Rubber lagging on the pulley raises μ for this reason[20].

Concept animation: tight side, slack side and slip at the drive pulley New · for review

The pulley can only pull the belt by friction. Tension falls from T1 on the incoming (tight) side to T2 on the outgoing (slack) side, and the capstan equation caps the ratio at T1/T2 ≤ eμθ. Lower the slack tension below about 14.4 N with the worked-check numbers and the belt slips: the pulley keeps turning but the belt lags. Both spans pull the shaft the same way, so the shaft carries T1 + T2: about 51 N at the minimum tension, more when the belt is set tighter.

Worked check. One-kilogram boxes at 0.3 m spacing on a 2 m level bed, belt 0.5 kg/m, μ = 0.3 and v = 0.3 m/s. Then m′p = 3.33 kg/m and Te = 0.3 × 9.81 × 3.83 × 2 = 22.6 N. Belt power is 6.8 W, or 10.2 W with the 1.5 service factor. A 60 mm pulley needs 0.68 N·m and turns at 95.5 rpm, so a 300 rpm motor needs a 3.14 : 1 reduction. With μ = 0.3 at the pulley and 180° of wrap, e0.3π = 2.57, so the slack side needs at least 14.4 N and the pulley shaft carries about 51 N.

The reel shows mass flow for loose bulk material as ṁ = ρAv, which is correct for a belt carrying sand or gravel. Its on-screen units are wrong, though. A is the cross-section area in m² and v is in m/s. This unit uses boxes because they are easier to count and weigh in a classroom.

Matching the motor to the load

Choose the motor and the ratio together. A DC motor at constant voltage slows in a straight line from its no-load speed to zero at its stall torque, and its mechanical power peaks at half the stall torque and half the no-load speed[43]. One gear motor maker advises running continuously below about 30% of stall torque, using 30% to 60% only for short periods, and staying out of the range above that[42]. Stall torque and no-load speed come from the motor's datasheet, or students can measure them.

Tmotor = Tload/(i · η)    Nmotor = N0(1 − Tmotor/Tstall)    Nout = Nmotor/i straight-line motor model[43]

Worked check. Take a gear motor with 2 N·m stall torque and 300 rpm no-load speed, the conveyor's 0.68 N·m pulley torque, a 3.14 : 1 reduction, and 90% gearbox efficiency (replace this with your module 1 measurement). The motor carries 12% of its stall torque, slows to 264 rpm, and turns the pulley at 84 rpm, well short of the 95.5 rpm the conveyor needs. Choosing the ratio from the loaded speed instead gives about 2.70 : 1, which lands at 95.6 rpm. Students who size the ratio from the no-load speed build a conveyor that runs about 12% slow.

Activities

  • Period 27Requirements. Teams turn the brief into a requirements sheet with numbers and pass/fail tests for each. NGSS asks that criteria be quantified so one can tell whether a design meets them[29]. Weigh the boxes and choose a speed and spacing.
  • Period 28Belt pull. Measure μ between candidate belt materials and the bed with the ramp method. Compute Te and power by hand, then check with the interactive.
  • Period 29Drive train. Choose pulley diameter, compute pulley speed and torque, and pick the motor and ratio together with the motor interactive so the loaded speed hits the target.   • Period 30CAD. Model the frame, pulleys, shaft and guard. Teams that brace their stand can check one side frame in the Truss Stress Visualizer. The reel's point applies here. CAD comes after the numbers, so the model reflects decisions already justified on paper.
  • Period 31Drawing and review. One dimensioned drawing of the drive assembly plus a bill of materials. Two teams swap packages and check each other's calculations before anyone cuts material.
  • Period 32 and 33Build.
  • Period 34Test. Time boxes over a measured distance for belt speed, check for slip at start-up, and measure motor current and voltage if you have the equipment. Record predicted versus measured for speed, pull and power.
  • Period 35Validation talks. Each team presents its requirements, the three biggest prediction gaps, what caused them, and what they would change in a second build. This covers evaluating a solution against criteria and trade-offs[29].

Materials. Belt material (rubber or fabric-backed belting, or wide timing belt), pulleys or rollers, the class gearmotors and gear sets from module 1, shafts and bearings, a flat bed (plywood, or UHMW sheet if available), extrusion or wood for the frame, boxes of known mass, a stopwatch, and a multimeter if you plan to measure electrical power.

Safety. The spot where a belt runs onto a pulley is an in-running nip that can pull in fingers, hair or sleeves. Guard both pulleys before first power-up, keep the switch within reach of the operator, and never adjust belt tension while the motor has power.

Math and physics supplements

Each supplement is a 10 to 20 minute mini-lesson for students who need it, assigned from the period 1 diagnostic or as they hit a wall. They can run as a station during work time while other teams build. Each has a quick check that works as an exit ticket.

S1. Units and conversions

Newton (N) for force, meter (m) for length, kilogram (kg) for mass, second (s) for time, joule (J) for energy and watt (W) for power. Two conversions come up constantly. 1 N·m = 1000 N·mm, and 1 MPa = 1 N/mm², because a pascal is 1 N/m² and there are a million square millimeters in a square meter. Working in N and mm gives stress directly in MPa. For speed, multiply rpm by 2π/60 to get rad/s, so 300 rpm is 31.4 rad/s[27]. The Gear Train Workbench reports torque in N·cm, and 1 N·m = 100 N·cm. For US customary sources, 1 inch is exactly 25.4 mm and 1 pound-force is 4.448 N.

Quick check. Convert 150 rpm to rad/s (15.7 rad/s), and 85 N·cm to N·m (0.85 N·m).

Used in every module.

S2. Rearranging a formula

Undo operations in reverse order. To solve τ = 16T/(πd³) for d, multiply both sides by d³, divide by τ, then take the cube root, giving d = ∛(16T/(πτ)). For T = 10,000 N·mm and τ = 50 MPa, d = ∛1018.6 = 10.1 mm.

Quick check. Solve P = τω for ω.

Used in modules 1, 2, 4 and 5.

S3. Ratios and proportions

A 12-tooth gear driving a 36-tooth gear is a 3 : 1 reduction. Speed divides by 3 and ideal torque multiplies by 3. With two stages, multiply the ratios, so 3 : 1 followed by 4 : 1 gives 12 : 1.

Quick check. A 20-tooth gear at 450 rpm drives a 60-tooth gear. Output speed? (150 rpm)

Used in modules 1 and 5.

S4. Right-triangle trig (tangent and inverse tangent)

In a right triangle, the tangent of an angle is the opposite side over the adjacent side, and the inverse tangent turns that ratio back into an angle[28]. Three uses in this unit. A thread with lead 4 mm on an 18 mm mean diameter has tan λ = 4/(π × 18) = 0.0707, so λ = 4.05°. A friction coefficient of 0.15 gives φ = tan⁻¹ 0.15 = 8.53°. A 20° pressure angle gives Fr = Ft tan 20° = 0.364 Ft.

Quick check. A block starts sliding when the ramp reaches 20°. Estimate μs. (0.36)

Used in modules 1 and 2.

S5. Powers, roots and the cube law

Shaft stress depends on d³. Doubling the diameter multiplies the cube by 2³ = 8, so the same torque produces one eighth of the stress. A 20% larger diameter gives 1.2³ = 1.73 times the strength. For the capstan extension, ex is a calculator key, and e0.3π = 2.57.

Quick check. A shaft grows from 10 mm to 15 mm. By what factor does its torque capacity grow? (3.375)

Used in modules 4 and 5.

S6. Mass, weight and free-body diagrams

Mass is how much stuff, in kg. Weight is the force gravity puts on it, W = mg, so 1 kg weighs 9.81 N. On a level surface the normal force equals the weight, which is how OpenStax gets a 980 N normal force for a 100 kg crate[22]. A free-body diagram shows one object and every force on it, drawn as arrows from where they act. The free-body inspector in the Truss Stress Visualizer draws this diagram for any joint and checks that the forces add to zero[40].

Quick check. Draw the forces on a box sitting still on a conveyor belt.

Used in every module.

S7. Torque, moments and balance

Torque is force times the perpendicular distance from the pivot[24]. An object is balanced against rotation when the torques add to zero, with counterclockwise counted positive and clockwise negative by convention[24]. A 100 N push 0.4 m from a pivot balances 400 N at 0.1 m on the other side. The same rule finds bearing reactions on a shaft and the shoe force on a brake lever.

Quick check. A 2 m shaft rests on bearings at each end with a 600 N gear load 0.5 m from the left bearing. Find both reactions. (450 N left, 150 N right)

Used in modules 0, 3 and 4.

S8. Friction

Static friction grows to match a push up to a maximum of μsN. Once sliding starts, kinetic friction is μkN, and μk is usually smaller[22]. Tilt a ramp until an object slides at steady speed and μk = tan θ[22]. OpenStax notes that published friction coefficients are only good to one or two digits[22], which is a good reason to measure your own.

Quick check. A 5 kg box on a bed with μ = 0.3. What force keeps it sliding? (14.7 N)

Used in modules 2, 3 and 5.

S9. Work, energy and power

Work is force times distance. Power is work per unit time, so it is also force times speed, or torque times angular speed[27]. Moving objects carry kinetic energy, ½mv² for straight-line motion and ½Iω² for spinning[25]. Lifting 2 kg by 0.5 m in 2 s takes (2 × 9.81 × 0.5)/2 = 4.9 W.

Quick check. A belt pulls with 20 N at 0.5 m/s. Power? (10 W)

Used in modules 0, 1, 3 and 5.

S10. Stress, strength and safety factor

Stress is force spread over an area, σ = F/A[23]. A 2000 N push on a rod with 50 mm² of cross-section is 40 MPa. A material's strength comes from its datasheet. The safety factor is strength divided by actual stress, so a factor of 2 means the part could take twice the load before yielding.

Quick check. A 10 mm diameter rod carries 3000 N. Stress? (38.2 MPa)

Used in modules 2, 4 and 5.

S11. Heat and specific heat

Heat needed for a temperature change is Q = mcΔT. The specific heat c depends on the material, and for iron and steel it is 452 J/(kg·°C)[26]. Putting 3000 J into 1 kg of steel raises it by 6.6 °C.

Quick check. The same 3000 J goes into a 0.2 kg steel brake pad holder. Temperature rise? (33 °C)

Used in module 3.
